🎣 Phishing Awareness Program

Defending the Human Firewall

📖 Module 1: Identifying the Bait (Spotting Fakes)

Cybercriminals go to great lengths to make malicious communications look legitimate. Here is how to spot the cracks in their disguise:

  • Display Name Spoofing: The sender's name might say "IT Support," but hovering over the name reveals an address like support@gmail-alerts.com instead of your company's domain.
  • Generic Greetings: Legitimate organizations usually address you by name. Be wary of "Dear Customer" or "Dear User."
  • Unexpected Attachments: Invoices or shipping documents you didn't request—especially .zip, .exe, or .docm files—are major red flags.
  • Check the URL: Look closely at the domain. Hackers use typosquatting (e.g., www.micros0ft.com).
  • SSL is Not a Guarantee: A padlock icon (HTTPS) only means the connection is encrypted; scammers secure their fake sites, too.

🧠 Module 2: The Psychology of a Hack

Social engineering is the art of manipulating people so they give up confidential information. Attackers rely on human emotions:

  • Urgency & Fear: "Your account will be suspended in 24 hours if you do not verify your details."
  • Authority: An email appearing to be from the CEO demanding an urgent wire transfer (Business Email Compromise).
  • Familiarity & Trust: Spoofing the identity of a known vendor you frequently do business with.
  • Curiosity: Sending an email titled "Q3 Salary Adjustments.xlsx" to tempt clicks.

📂 Module 3: Real-World Case Studies

Case Study 1: Google & Facebook ($100 Million Loss)

Between 2013 and 2015, a hacker set up a fake company impersonating an Asian hardware vendor. He sent forged invoices and phishing emails. Because the name was familiar, employees bypassed verification, wiring over $100 million to offshore accounts.

Case Study 2: Ubiquiti Networks ($46.7 Million Loss)

In 2015, attackers impersonated executives and instructed the finance department to make urgent, secretive wire transfers. The employees, pressured by "authority," complied without verifying over the phone.

🛡️ Module 4: Practical Defense

To protect yourself and your organization, adopt the S.L.A.M. verification method:

  • S - Sender: Always check the actual email address, not just the display name.
  • L - Links: Hover over links without clicking to preview the destination URL.
  • A - Attachments: Never open unexpected attachments.
  • M - Message: Look for typos, poor grammar, and urgent language.

📝 Module 5: Knowledge Check

Q1: You receive an email from IT-Support@company-helpdesk.com stating your password expires in 2 hours and provides a link to keep your current password. What should you do?

A) Click the link quickly before the 2 hours run out.
B) Reply to the email asking if it's real.
C) Report the email to your actual IT department and do not click.
View Answer

Correct Answer: C. This email uses a false sense of urgency and a suspicious domain name.

Q2: True or False: If a website has a padlock icon next to the URL (HTTPS), it is 100% safe to enter your credit card info.

A) True
B) False
View Answer

Correct Answer: B (False). Scammers easily obtain SSL certificates to make their fake sites look secure.

Q3: What is "Whaling"?

A) Phishing emails sent randomly to thousands of people.
B) A highly targeted phishing attack aimed at senior executives.
C) Sending malicious text messages.
View Answer

Correct Answer: B. Whaling targets the "big fish" like CEOs or CFOs who have high-level access.
